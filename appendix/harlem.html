Harlem: A case study about written and unwritten rules

Chapter 5  ·  Page 75

For over a century in Harlem, the unwritten rules of culture have outrun the written ones of policy in shaping who gets to live in the neighborhood. The unwritten rules of the market defined what was acceptable in practice then, and still do now.

In New York City’s Harlem, which is probably the most storied Black neighborhood in America, we can see the tension between written and unwritten rules play out.

Developers built a glut of homes in Harlem in the early 1900s, speculating that the extension of subway lines to run uptown would result in mass demand for homes among the (non-ethnic) white middle class. It would be their protected escape. The deeds for these homes included “racial covenants” that remain written into the deeds to this day—provisions explicitly stating that the properties cannot be sold to Black people and, in some cases, cannot even be occupied by Black people other than servants. (Racial covenants exist in deeds all across the country, too, targeting a wide variety of racial and ethnic groups.)

Yet, when the white homeowner boom never materialized, landlords and real estate agents looked to Black tenants and would-be homeowners to fill the empty blocks and save them financial loss. The resulting concentration of Black people in Harlem by the 1920s led to the Harlem Renaissance and became its own history.

The written rule of the deed was essentially nullified by the unwritten rule of the financial market, backed by its investors’ relentless drive for profit, which had a lot more force behind it at the time. That is, the unwritten rule presented a much more coercive force in terms of governing people’s decisions and behavior than any written rule, making what was officially not allowed suddenly allowable. That operative rule, “profit over all else,” continues to guide the business sector and shape its role in our lives.

Much later, New York passed written laws that officially nullified the racial covenant policies written into those deeds, even though their racist language remains. The written rules took a while to catch up to the unwritten rules.

Today, there’s another set of unwritten rules—gentrification—that is overpowering the written rules of housing policy, with the opposite effect. In essence, this new set of unwritten rules is reproducing the outcome of the old racial exclusion provisions written into the original deeds, as a real estate market organized around profit aggressively favors selling or renting units to those who can pay the most—increasingly, white people—in ways that displace the very Black people who can trace their roots in Harlem back more than a century and made it famous. Fair housing law prohibits discrimination, but it has less power over a market that sorts people by what they can pay. In the present, the unwritten rules of real estate culture are more determinative—more powerful and coercive—than the written rules of anti-discrimination policy.
