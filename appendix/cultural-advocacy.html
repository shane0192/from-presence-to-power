The Whitepaper on Cultural Advocacy

The 1992 GLAAD paper that proved highly influential for developing a strategic front in the fight for social change through the lever of culture.

In 1992, the Gay & Lesbian Alliance Against Defamation (GLAAD) published an internal whitepaper that would help define a generation of social change advocacy. The paper articulated, for the first time in systematic terms, the idea that cultural representation was not merely a symbolic goal but a strategic one—that changing how people are seen in media and entertainment was inseparable from changing how they are treated in law and in life.

The core argument of the whitepaper was deceptively simple: before you can change the laws, you have to change the culture; and before you can change the culture, you have to change the stories. What people believe about their neighbors, about their community, about who belongs in the category of "people like us" — these beliefs are shaped primarily not by policy debates or political speeches, but by the stories that circulate through culture: the films people watch, the television shows they follow, the music they listen to, the books they read.

GLAAD's whitepaper laid out a theory of change built around five core propositions:

  1. Representation precedes rights. Before a community can achieve political victories, it must achieve cultural visibility. People support the rights of those they see as fully human, as people like themselves. Dehumanization in culture—through stereotypes, caricature, absence, or othering—makes political dehumanization easier. Humanization in culture makes political inclusion more achievable.
  2. The goal is not just more images, but better ones. Visibility alone is not enough if the images being circulated reinforce negative stereotypes or reduce complex people to one-dimensional representations. The goal is full, complex, authentic representation—stories that show the breadth of a community's humanity.
  3. Cultural change requires sustained engagement, not one-off interventions. A single positive portrayal, a single film or television episode, does not change culture. Culture changes when the accumulation of representations shifts the baseline of what people expect and believe. This requires a long-term strategy, relationships with creators and producers, and the patience to work across many projects over many years.
  4. Creators are partners, not adversaries. The writers, directors, producers, and executives who make cultural products are not enemies of social change—they are potential allies. Many of them are members of marginalized communities themselves, or allies who want to tell better stories. The work of cultural advocacy is to support and enable these creators, to provide them with resources and relationships, and to make the case that good storytelling and authentic representation are not in tension but are deeply connected.
  5. Economic power matters. The entertainment industry responds to economic signals. When stories about a community attract audiences and generate revenue, the industry takes notice. Part of the work of cultural advocacy is to demonstrate the market for authentic stories—to show that there is an audience hungry for better representation, and that serving that audience is good for business.

These five propositions became, over the following decades, a kind of playbook not just for LGBTQ+ advocacy, but for racial justice, disability rights, gender equity, and many other movements. Color Of Change drew heavily on this framework in developing its own approach to cultural advocacy through Hollywood and the entertainment industry. The idea that changing stories changes culture, and that changing culture changes politics, has become one of the organizing principles of a generation of social change advocacy.

What makes the GLAAD whitepaper so remarkable is that it was produced at a time when the idea of cultural advocacy as a distinct strategic discipline was barely recognized. The civil rights movement had understood the power of images and stories—the power of photographs of police brutality, of Coretta Scott King's dignified presence, of the March on Washington's visual grandeur. But the systematic theorization of cultural strategy as a lever for social change, the idea that organizations should invest deliberately in shifting the stories that circulate through mass culture, was new.

The whitepaper drew on scholarship from cultural studies, on the work of people like Stuart Hall who had theorized the relationship between representation and power, and translated that scholarship into practical strategic guidance. It was, in the best sense, an act of translation—taking ideas from the academy and making them operational for advocates and organizers.

Today, cultural advocacy has become a recognized and sophisticated field. Organizations like Color Of Change, the National Hispanic Media Coalition, GLAAD, and many others have developed deep expertise in how to engage with the entertainment industry, how to build relationships with creators and executives, how to conduct research on representation, and how to translate that research into change. The theories articulated in the 1992 whitepaper have been tested, refined, and extended across decades of practice.

But the core insight remains: who tells the stories, whose stories get told, and how those stories are told — these questions are questions of power. And changing the answers to those questions is not separate from the work of winning political power and building a more just society. It is part of the same work.
