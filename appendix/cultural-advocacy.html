The Whitepaper on Cultural Advocacy

Chapter 15  ·  Page 286

The chapter references a white paper created in the early days of GLAAD that proved highly influential for developing a strategic front in the fight for social change that was separate from legal, policy, political, and organizing levers—namely, the lever of culture.

The paper I got from Joan Garry at GLAAD was titled “Cultural Advocacy: A Non-Legal Approach to Fighting Defamation of Lesbians and Gays.” It had been published in 1992 in a journal called Law & Sexuality: A Review of Lesbians & Gay Legal Issues. In the decades since it was put in my hands, so many papers and reports have been written about “culture change” strategies. But this work was seminal and is still worth reading.

The paper itself had an emotional story of its own. The initial author was Craig Davidson, a lawyer who could think beyond legal strategies. He had been the founding executive director of GLAAD and wanted to put down on paper what the early GLAAD activists had learned and had lived out through their actions. When he died in 1991, Michael Valentini, his partner, finished the paper as a tribute to him.

The main thesis was simple: There are ways to change attitudes toward gay and lesbian people without resorting to legislation or litigation, strategies that are coercive at their core and therefore very limited and susceptible to being overturned if there is a crack in the enforcement mechanism—as is true with anything based on forcing people to do things instead of bringing them around to genuinely believing in doing it. (Remember my example about the Wall Street banks and their political influence: “Buying off” politicians is not nearly as effective or rewarding, in the long run, as getting them to see the world through Wall Street eyes so that they come to the conclusions and decisions Wall Street wants them to make, all on their own.)

Davidson and Valentini argued it is possible to use the power of media to get people to believe that inclusion is good and exclusion is bad—to get them to want change rather than feeling like they were forced to accept it. But the rules of media would have to change in order to achieve that goal. They identified different types of defamation and how each needed to be confronted in different ways: slander, stereotypes, casual prejudice, deference (i.e., silence in the face of others’ slander, stereotypes, and prejudice), and forced invisibility. The first four focused on eliminating harmful content, and the fifth was about advancing helpful content.

They suggested a three-pronged approach to media cultural advocacy:

  1. Mobilizing the LGBTQ+ community to respond quickly to positive or negative portrayals.
  2. Working directly with media professionals to improve their understanding of the LGBTQ+ community.
  3. Promoting gay and lesbian visibility through media campaigns.

It was a methodical, strategic approach. It immediately resonated with me. It didn’t go deep on the analysis of narratives and norms, but it went deep on the tactics of effecting cultural change, especially by engaging producers of major media content in very pointed and intentional ways. A carrot-and-stick approach to taking on Hollywood may not seem novel now, but in 1991, and even in 2005 when I got to GLAAD, pairing that approach with the infrastructure needed to deliver on it was pioneering. The innovations that followed from GLAAD, Color Of Change, the National Domestic Workers Alliance, Define American, and others has made deep impacts.
