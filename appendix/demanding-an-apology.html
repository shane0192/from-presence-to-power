Appendix

Demanding an apology: When it works and when it doesn’t

Chapter 4  ·  Page 63

Apologies are an easy thing for corporations to give, which is exactly why they are so often what we get. This passage lays out what each demand can and cannot accomplish, so we can tell the difference between a cost that actually changes structural practice, and a cost they were always prepared to pay.

There are a lot of good reasons to lash out at corporations, and a lot of ways to do it. We may want restitution for an individual harm, like a corporation knowingly putting a faulty product on the market and hurting someone, and so we call out a corporation publicly or sue them to get compensated. We may want to prevent a specific harm, like corporations dumping toxic waste into public waterways or using toxic chemicals that hurt people in their production processes, and so we call for investigations and regulations to rein them in. Or we may want to use a corporation as a punching bag, as conservatives do in their culture wars. And so we organize a boycott, make fun of them in the media, or use them as an example to rally people.

On the other hand, we may want a corporation to announce its support of a cause or send charitable dollars in its direction. And so we meet with its leaders and make the case, while creating a media environment that incentivizes them to get on board with that cause—providing some kind of meaningful reward for doing so.

All of those reasons for confronting corporations, and all those ways of doing it (many of which I have engaged in myself), are completely valid and extremely important. They can lead to important and useful specific outcomes and perhaps, if part of the right larger strategy, create valuable momentum toward systemic change. A corporation can apologize. It can give a donation to a charity. It can give an individual person compensation for their troubles. It can change its language. Those are not necessarily bad outcomes, but they are not systemic outcomes in themselves.

The real challenge in the domain of social change is maintaining pressure long enough and well enough to convert it into real consequences—forcing a corporation to change its business model and thereby change its practices. That is what can prevent the otherwise persistent harms the corporation causes: child labor, discrimination, worker exploitation, health and environmental destruction, consumer gouging, and so much more. That’s what it takes to prevent major profiteering from injustice in any form.

Corporations can use an individual apology, fine or other concession to smooth over public angst, all in service of going back to exactly what they were doing before they were called out. If they can write it off as the cost of doing business, rather than a mandate for changing how they do business, it’s not a real win. It’s critical to know the difference.
